# War for Mars: Infinite War — Multiplayer Universe Architecture & Development Assessment

What you're describing is considerably bigger than adding multiplayer to War for Mars. You're describing an interconnected multiplayer gaming universe in which multiple independent competitive games, live battles, space stations, operations, and player communities exist inside one persistent, explorable universe.

I think this is the right direction for the evolution of War for Mars.

The important distinction is that we should not build several disconnected multiplayer games and put them behind a menu. We should build one overarching multiplayer universe that hosts multiple playable experiences as locations, activities, and operations within that universe.

Your asteroid example is particularly important because it establishes a new design principle:

The universe itself becomes a living multiplayer game lobby.

A player flying through space should be able to discover another group of players engaged in a competitive match, see the battle happening, approach the location, observe it, and eventually join or enter the competition.

That is a much more ambitious experience than conventional matchmaking.

# WAR FOR MARS: INFINITE WAR

## One universe. Multiple battle systems. One connected multiplayer ecosystem.

The universe contains:

- A persistent open-world space environment.
- Independently operating multiplayer battle arenas.
- Asteroid-based competitive operations.
- Orbital combat stations.
- Cooperative missions and Wing operations.
- Legacy flight simulation competitions.
- Campaigns, arcade experiences, and exploration.
- Live events visible to other players traveling through the universe.

I would establish five architectural principles:

1. One universe: Every experience belongs to the same overarching world.
2. Multiple game engines: Different experiences can retain their own specialized simulation and gameplay runtimes.
3. Shared identity: Players, progression, competition records, rewards, and matchmaking identities are connected.
4. Live world visibility: Activities generate observable events in the universe.
5. Independent scalability: A competitive match can operate independently without destabilizing the open-world simulation.

### What is not implemented yet

Major multiplayer integration still outstanding

The repository's own architecture assessment makes an important distinction:

The new orbital combat engine is not yet the live multiplayer engine for the entire universe.

The following systems remain distinct:

1. Orbital Combat Lab: offline training.
2. Rust Disc: its existing flight and exploration runtime.
3. Campaign: server-issued runs and replay validation.
4. Cooperative sectors: authenticated multiplayer socket operations.
5. Online Wing: its existing competitive 5v5 planar combat system.

The ten-seat orbital authority harness is not a ten-human multiplayer server. It does not yet establish authenticated players controlling ships together in the new 3D orbital environment.

This is precisely where your new asteroid-based multiplayer concept should enter the development plan.

The architecture must progress from a collection of working systems toward a shared universe that can host and display independently operating multiplayer experiences.

### Scenario: A player discovers an active asteroid competition

1. Universe exploration

   A player is flying through a 3D sector toward Phobos Yard. Their navigation system identifies an asteroid containing an active USSF-versus-Accord competition.
2. Live battle visibility

   As the player approaches, they can see spacecraft moving around the asteroid, firing weapons, launching missiles, and engaging opposing teams.

   The battle is not a prerecorded animation. It is a visual representation of an actual active multiplayer session.
3. Approach and observe

   The player can fly closer, inspect the battle, see its status, and select an available spectator or participation opportunity.
4. Enter the asteroid

   The player flies toward an entrance, docking structure, or designated deployment corridor.

   The game transitions them into the asteroid's competitive operation instance.
5. Join the competition

   If eligible and a seat is available, the player enters the appropriate team lobby, receives a server-authorized assignment, and joins the actual match.
6. Complete the competition

   The match has its own objectives, combat simulation, authoritative server, results, and eligible progression.
7. Return to the universe

   The player returns to the asteroid or nearby space, and the operation's visible status updates to reflect its actual completion.

This should work in reverse too: players already competing should be able to see the surrounding universe and, where the game mode allows, see other spacecraft approaching their asteroid.

### The technical secret: two connected simulation layers

We should not attempt to render every bullet, projectile, and movement calculation from every multiplayer match to every player in the galaxy.

That would become unnecessarily expensive.

Instead, build two connected layers.

### Three levels of battle visibility

I would make this a formal system:

- Galaxy visibility: An operation marker identifies an active event.
- Local visibility: Approaching players receive interpolated spacecraft positions, combat effects, and live event status.
- Spectator visibility: Authorized observers receive a more detailed, read-only representation of the match.

Players should not receive hidden opponent information, unrevealed tactical data, or unrestricted competitive information through spectator subscriptions.

## 6. How to preserve your existing engines and migrate the games

I agree with keeping the original flight simulation available as a separate experience.

In fact, I recommend that we distinguish three things:

- The overarching universe platform.
- The canonical orbital combat simulation.
- Specialized legacy game experiences.

They should communicate through explicit interfaces rather than being forced into one simulation implementation.

The original Rust Disc flight simulation can remain a specialized competitive or training experience inside the new universe. The player does not have to abandon its existing gameplay to enter the larger Infinite War.

Recommended migration sequence

Staged migration

1. Rust Disc integration

   Introduce the canonical orbital combat foundation through a compatibility adapter. Preserve the original flight runtime as a fallback and retain its separate gameplay identity.
2. Remaining Arcade and Campaign modes

   Migrate one operation at a time. Use versioned simulation rules, preserve mission objectives, and validate replay and progression parity.
3. Cooperative multiplayer

   Connect the new combat foundation to Rust Disc's existing server-authoritative cooperative sectors and Iron Eclipse. Preserve squadron AI, commands, objectives, authentication, and discovery settlement.
4. Competitive Wing

   Migrate the existing planar 5v5 competitive system to the new 3D orbital protocol through a coordinated client/API/socket rollout.

   Preserve ten distinct authenticated humans, team balance, reconnect, competitive eligibility, and the existing reward ledger.
5. Orbital Operations Network

   Connect the migrated experiences to asteroid destinations, live operation discovery, spectator visibility, and instance orchestration.
6. Expansion

   Introduce tournaments, multiple simultaneous arenas, larger fleet battles, faction events, and synchronized universe-wide operations.

I would establish the operation directory and its location/event contracts early, then activate each game experience through it as that experience becomes genuinely multiplayer-capable. That prevents us from building several unrelated systems and trying to connect them afterward.

## 7. The important gaps we need AI to solve

After inspecting the existing implementation, I see several concrete gaps between today's architecture and your proposed multiplayer universe.

| Capability                  | Existing foundation                             | Required development                            |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- |
| 3D orbital flight           | Combat Lab                                      | Live authenticated multiplayer                  |
| Asteroid destinations       | Existing world locations and spatial simulation | Physical, navigable multiplayer arena locations |
| Active match discovery      | Existing world API                              | Live operation directory                        |
| Nearby battle visibility    | Existing 3D renderer                            | Observer-safe live event rendering              |
| Spectator mode              | Not established for orbital combat              | Read-only spectator protocol                    |
| Match entry                 | Existing Wing and co-op sessions                | Universe-to-instance handoff                    |
| Multiple simultaneous games | Separate modes and rooms                        | Shared operation orchestration                  |
| Persistent consequences     | Existing progression and world state            | Validated operation-to-universe updates         |

### Three problems deserve particular attention

1\. The universe is not yet a live multiplayer environment.

The current `world.ts` provides locations, travel, discoveries, and other account-backed world information. `GalaxyScreen.tsx` refreshes its world snapshot periodically.

That is useful groundwork, but it is not an event-streaming system that lets you see actual multiplayer battles occurring at distant locations.

2\. The new orbital authority needs a real multiplayer connection.

`orbital-authority.ts` is currently an isolated server testing harness. It must eventually become part of an authenticated, session-managed multiplayer architecture.

It must not simply be exposed as a public socket endpoint.

3\. The games need a common integration contract.

This is arguably the biggest architectural challenge.

We need every game experience to communicate with the universe without requiring every experience to have identical physics, controls, or gameplay.

For example, Rust Disc can preserve its specialized flight simulation while an asteroid dogfight uses the newer orbital combat simulation.

Both should understand:

- Who the player is.
- Which universe location they occupy.
- Which operation they are entering.
- Which ship and loadout they have.
- How their match is authorized.
- How the result is validated.
- How they return to the universe.

This is what the proposed Operation Adapter solves.

## 8. The master AI development prompt

This is the prompt I would give the development AI.

It is intentionally more demanding than a conventional feature request. It instructs the AI to act as a principal multiplayer architect, game-engine engineer, network engineer, and forensic software auditor.

It also explicitly prevents the AI from taking shortcuts such as building a visual mockup and claiming that real multiplayer has been implemented.

Recommended development directive

# MASTER DEVELOPMENT DIRECTIVE

# WAR FOR MARS: INFINITE WAR

## THE CONNECTED MULTIPLAYER UNIVERSE, ORBITAL OPERATIONS NETWORK & ASTEROID BATTLE SYSTEM

**Repository:** [https://github.com/niakofa-cmyk/WFM](https://github.com/niakofa-cmyk/WFM)

**Primary product:** WAR FOR MARS: INFINITE WAR

**Development foundation:** The established Orbital Combat Lab and the existing custom Three.js/Vite runtime.

**Primary directive:** Transform War for Mars into one interconnected, expandable multiplayer universe capable of hosting multiple independent competitive, cooperative, campaign, arcade, and flight-simulation experiences within one shared universe.

---

# 1. UNDERSTAND THE COMPLETE VISION BEFORE WRITING CODE

You are not being asked to add another game mode, another menu, another isolated arena, or another multiplayer lobby.

You are being asked to architect and implement an overarching multiplayer universe.

The universe must become the central game environment through which players discover, approach, enter, participate in, observe, and leave multiple independently operating multiplayer experiences.

The defining product principle is:

**THE UNIVERSE IS THE BATTLEFIELD. EVERY COMPETITIVE ENGAGEMENT IS AN OPERATION WITHIN THE LARGER WAR. EVERY OPERATION CAN HAVE CONSEQUENCES FOR THE UNIVERSE.**

The player must experience one connected game, not a collection of disconnected game commands.

Before implementation, inspect and understand:

- The complete Infinite War blueprint.
- The Master Development Directive.
- The Structural Rebuild Roadmap.
- The existing Orbital Combat Lab.
- The shared orbital simulation package.
- Rust Disc and its original flight simulation.
- The remaining Arcade and Campaign modes.
- Cooperative sectors and Operation Iron Eclipse.
- Existing 5v5 Online Wing matchmaking.
- Clerk authentication and account ownership.
- Existing socket authentication and reconnect protocols.
- World, Galaxy, travel, progression, inventory, and economy systems.
- Railway deployment architecture and staging safeguards.
- All existing automated tests and release evidence.

Read the actual implementation. Do not infer capabilities from documentation alone.

Establish the exact current staging commit and distinguish implemented, partially implemented, simulated, locally tested, staging-verified, and Production-verified functionality.

# 2. BUILD THE ORBITAL OPERATIONS NETWORK

Create a foundational subsystem named:

**ORBITAL OPERATIONS NETWORK (OON)**

This is the infrastructure that connects multiplayer activities to the larger universe.

It must support:

1. Asteroid-based competitive arenas.
2. Permanent orbital multiplayer stations.
3. Cooperative mission destinations.
4. Flight-simulation competitions.
5. Arcade and campaign operations.
6. Temporary multiplayer events.
7. Faction battles and fleet engagements.
8. Future tournaments and large-scale multiplayer experiences.

Every operation must have a defined identity, location, ruleset, lifecycle, player capacity, visibility policy, and server-authoritative session.

Develop an explicit operation contract that can support different game engines and simulation runtimes.

Evaluate and define the appropriate interfaces for:

- OperationDefinition.
- OperationInstance.
- UniverseLocation.
- UniverseEvent.
- PlayerPresence.
- MatchAssignment.
- SpectatorSession.
- JoinAuthorization.
- OperationResult.

Do not create duplicate player identity, account, inventory, progression, or reward systems.

Reuse existing authoritative services and database contracts wherever possible.

# 3. BUILD THE ASTEROID MULTIPLAYER EXPERIENCE

This is a primary gameplay requirement.

Create actual asteroid destinations within the 3D universe.

These asteroids must be physical locations that players can navigate toward and approach.

Each designated asteroid may host one or more multiplayer operations.

The player must be able to:

1. Discover the asteroid while exploring.
2. See its location and operational status.
3. Navigate toward it using real 3D flight.
4. Observe actual battles happening around it.
5. Approach the combat area.
6. Inspect available competitions.
7. Enter a spectator experience.
8. Dock or enter a designated operation entrance.
9. Join a match when authorized and eligible.
10. Complete the competition.
11. Return to the larger universe.

Implement the asteroid as a real navigable universe destination, not a static background image or disconnected menu button.

Use original WFM environments and the established procedural orbital assets where appropriate.

# 4. IMPLEMENT LIVE MULTIPLAYER EVENT VISIBILITY

One of the most important requirements is that players must be able to SEE actual multiplayer battles occurring elsewhere in the universe.

For example:

A player is flying through an asteroid field.

An asteroid nearby is hosting an active USSF-versus-Accord competition.

The player should see actual participating spacecraft moving, turning, accelerating, firing weapons, launching missiles, taking damage, and being destroyed.

The visible event must reflect a genuine active multiplayer session.

Implement three visibility levels:

**LEVEL 1 — GALAXY DISCOVERY**

Display active operations, destinations, faction involvement, and availability.

**LEVEL 2 — LOCAL EVENT RENDERING**

As players approach an active operation, subscribe them to an appropriate interest-managed event stream.

Render observer-safe spacecraft proxies, movement, combat effects, and actual event status.

Use interpolation and bounded update frequencies. Do not replicate unnecessary full combat state to every universe player.

**LEVEL 3 — SPECTATOR EXPERIENCE**

Provide an authorized read-only spectator session.

Support appropriate camera perspectives, team observation, match status, and event progression.

Prevent spectator connections from exposing hidden tactical information, unrevealed opponent state, private player information, or competitive advantages.

The spectator system must never grant control over an active competitive participant.

Most importantly, do not create prerecorded animations or simulated player activity and represent them as live users.

Development fixtures must be explicitly identified as test fixtures.

# 5. BUILD A REAL MULTI-INSTANCE MULTIPLAYER ARCHITECTURE

The universe must support multiple independently operating multiplayer sessions.

Do not attempt to put every player and every match inside one enormous simulation process.

Implement a bounded, scalable instance architecture.

Evaluate:

- Session orchestration.
- Matchmaking.
- Room creation and lifecycle.
- Region and server allocation.
- Authenticated player admission.
- Team assignment.
- Live state replication.
- Interest management.
- Reconnection and session recovery.
- Spectator subscriptions.
- Match completion and cleanup.
- Server health and capacity management.

Inspect the existing Railway deployment topology before selecting an implementation.

Do not introduce Redis, a new database, a new authentication provider, or additional infrastructure without demonstrating why the existing architecture cannot safely support the requirement.

The architecture should allow:

Asteroid A — competitive 5v5.

Asteroid B — cooperative capital-ship operation.

Orbital Station C — flight-simulation tournament.

Asteroid D — another competitive match.

Temporary Event E — faction invasion.

All should be capable of operating independently while sharing the same overarching universe identity and operation directory.

# 6. PRESERVE SPECIALIZED GAME ENGINES

Do not destroy the original Rust Disc flight simulation.

The objective is to integrate its experience into the new Infinite War architecture, not unnecessarily erase its identity or working mechanics.

Establish a compatibility adapter between the universe operation system and the legacy flight simulation.

The legacy experience must remain accessible as a specialized side experience or independent operation.

Keep the newer Orbital Combat Lab as the evolving canonical orbital combat foundation.

Where simulation migration is appropriate, use versioned rules and prove client/server parity.

Where preserving an independent simulation is safer, preserve it.

Do not force different game experiences into identical physics or controls simply to claim they share an engine.

One universe does not require one monolithic simulation.

# 7. FOLLOW THE REQUIRED MIGRATION SEQUENCE

Execute the migration in this order.

**STAGE A — RUST DISC**

- Audit both the original local Rust Disc trainer and the authenticated cooperative Rust Disc sector.
- Explicitly distinguish the two.
- Identify all existing gameplay, flight, weapons, collision, mission, and progression behavior.
- Connect the local flight experience to the new orbital foundation through a compatibility adapter.
- Preserve the legacy runtime as a fallback.
- Verify the actual player journey and mission completion.

**STAGE B — REMAINING ARCADE AND CAMPAIGN**

- Inventory every existing arcade and campaign experience.
- Migrate one experience at a time.
- Preserve story progression, mission objectives, ship behavior, and server-issued runs.
- Version simulation contracts.
- Maintain legacy replay compatibility.
- Prove server/client replay parity.
- Preserve existing claim and idempotency safeguards.

**STAGE C — LIVE COOPERATIVE MULTIPLAYER**

- Connect orbital simulation to authenticated cooperative operations.
- Preserve Rust Disc → Mars Approach → Pale Beacons.
- Preserve Iron Eclipse.
- Preserve squadron AI, host commands, capital-ship subsystems, extraction, and mission objectives.
- Ensure server-owned damage and completion.
- Preserve existing zero-credit discovery settlement unless a separately approved reward policy is introduced.
- Verify reconnection and interruption behavior.

**STAGE D — COMPETITIVE WING**

Migrate the existing 5v5 competitive system into the new orbital multiplayer architecture.

Preserve:

- Clerk authentication.
- Ten distinct authenticated humans.
- Balanced USSF and Accord teams.
- Server-authoritative flight and damage.
- Competitive queue eligibility.
- Reconnect and account ownership.
- Existing reward policy.
- Transactional and idempotent reward settlement.
- Private-room and AI-fill restrictions.
- Feature flags and release safeguards.

Do not enable competitive orbital rewards merely because the orbital simulation works.

A successful migration requires actual ten-human staging verification.

**STAGE E — UNIVERSE OPERATIONS**

Connect the migrated experiences to the Orbital Operations Network.

Implement asteroid destinations, operation discovery, live event visibility, spectator sessions, docking, matchmaking, instance transitions, and return-to-universe functionality.

**STAGE F — EXPANSION**

Introduce additional simultaneous operations, tournaments, larger fleet battles, faction events, synchronized anomalies, and new universe locations.

Expand based on measured server capacity and mobile performance.

# 8. BUILD THE SHARED OPERATION ADAPTER

Develop a common interface allowing different game experiences to communicate with the universe.

Each experience must be capable of:

- Receiving an authenticated player identity.
- Receiving the selected ship and eligible loadout.
- Receiving an authorized operation assignment.
- Starting the appropriate simulation runtime.
- Reporting validated operation state.
- Producing a server-owned completion result.
- Returning the player to the universe.
- Updating eligible existing progression systems.

Support independent simulation implementations behind versioned adapters.

The universe should not need to understand every internal mechanic of every game.

It needs a consistent operation lifecycle and trusted results.

# 9. PROTECT SERVER AUTHORITY AND EXISTING SYSTEMS

This requirement is non-negotiable.

Clients may render, predict, animate, and transmit validated control inputs.

Clients must never decide:

- Competitive damage.
- Ship destruction.
- Match outcomes.
- Mission completion.
- Player identity.
- Team assignment.
- Rewards.
- Credits.
- Persistent faction consequences.

Preserve existing authentication and session verification.

Reject forged, malformed, stale, unauthorized, and replayed inputs.

Prevent duplicate account ownership across simultaneous competitive seats.

Maintain existing reconnect and shutdown behavior.

Ensure duplicate completion requests cannot create duplicate progression or rewards.

Do not weaken security checks to make a prototype appear functional.

# 12. DEVELOPMENT AND RELEASE SAFEGUARDS

Use the current staging architecture.

First inspect the current staging branch, latest commit, outstanding pull requests, and existing Railway services.

Keep Production untouched.

Do not:

- Merge directly into main.
- Bypass required CI checks.
- Skip staging verification.
- Disable authentication.
- Remove existing multiplayer safeguards.
- Replace the database.
- Introduce duplicate reward systems.

Each pull request must have a defined rollback path.

# 13. AI ENGINEERING EXPECTATIONS

Operate as a principal-level game architect and engineering team.

Think beyond the immediate code change.

Challenge architectural assumptions. Identify missing systems. Discover integration opportunities. Evaluate alternative technical approaches. Investigate performance and scalability risks.

Do not settle for the easiest superficial implementation.

Do not produce another isolated prototype when the requirement is integration into the existing game.

Do not mistake a menu, static animation, synthetic player, or locally simulated battle for a functioning multiplayer system.

Use the repository's actual existing infrastructure as the foundation.

When a feature cannot safely be completed in one stage, establish its complete architecture and implement the next independently testable vertical slice.

Do not stop at saying something is difficult. Find a technically sound, measurable path to implement it.

However, never bypass security, testing, release gates, or existing data protections in pursuit of speed.

After the audit, implement the smallest safe, demonstrable asteroid-operation vertical slice on a new branch based on the current staging source.

Do not claim live combat or multiplayer spectator functionality until actual participants and synchronized server state have been demonstrated.

**G. Final Engineering Report**

Report exact commit, changed files, tests, build results, browser evidence, staging status, limitations, and next development stage.

## 10. My final recommendation

I believe this concept could become one of the most distinctive parts of War for Mars.

The most interesting aspect isn't necessarily the asteroid arena itself. It is the fact that multiplayer becomes part of the environment rather than something players have to leave the environment to access.

Consider the future possibilities:

- You fly past a space station and see a tournament underway.
- You encounter a distress signal from a cooperative mission.
- You discover an asteroid hosting an ongoing faction war.
- You observe a fleet battle before deciding whether to join.
- You see a temporary invasion developing in a neighboring sector.
- You travel to a location where several different competitions are taking place.
- You participate in one operation, return to space, and immediately continue exploring.

That is the foundation of a living multiplayer universe.

My recommendation is to retain the custom Three.js foundation, preserve the original Rust Disc simulation, and make the Orbital Operations Network the next major architectural development.
